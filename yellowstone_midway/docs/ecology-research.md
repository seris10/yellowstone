# Midway Geyser Basin and the Firehole: ecological research and implementation guide

Research reviewed **7 October 2026**. Geographic scope: Grand Prismatic Spring, Excelsior Geyser Crater, the adjacent Firehole River, the Grand Loop Road corridor, and nearby forest and meadow. This is a research and authoring specification. It does **not** certify that every recommended organism is present in the delivered scene, that every plausible species has been recorded at this exact site, or that the proposed model coefficients are calibrated.

The accompanying `data/species_catalogue.json` separates source evidence, placement eligibility, and proposed simulation roles. A species catalogue is a list of candidates and constraints, not a command to populate the landscape with every species on the list.

## Delivered implementation and its limits

The research below has been used to build a deliberately small working subset. [`scripts/ecosystem.gd`](../scripts/ecosystem.gd) reads [`data/ecosystem.json`](../data/ecosystem.json). Six serial Firehole reaches exchange heat and carry the same water downstream, with one explicitly added Excelsior source. The transport clock uses physical seconds; a one-second integration step is subdivided further if a configured reach needs it. An independently calculated domain ledger accounts for upstream input, lateral input, terminal export, and an illustrative atmospheric heat-exchange term. The model retains elapsed-time debt after slow frames instead of dropping it.

**These are uncalibrated demonstration states.** Boundary discharge, river volumes, entry temperature after Excelsior outflow cooling, atmospheric exchange coefficients, and fish response coefficients have provenance notes in the JSON. The NPS description of Excelsior supports the order of magnitude of the source, but does not validate the entire simulated reach network. There is no bathymetric reconstruction, two-dimensional plume solver, dissolved-oxygen solver, complete tributary inventory, or validated fish refuge map. Adjusting the clock phase changes the lighting and simplified weather boundary; it does not integrate hours of river transport in one frame.

[`scripts/wildlife_actor.gd`](../scripts/wildlife_actor.gd) constructs twelve explicit actor records: three bison, two Canada geese, one American dipper, one raven, four introduced trout, and one group of eight tiny insect proxies. The animals have original articulated geometric forms at approximate real dimensions. Their exact individual locations, numbers, and movements are authored representations. They are not wildlife observations or finished photographic assets. Bison alternate grazing, short walks and occasional rests. Their actual grazing states debit the corresponding forage index. Dipper foraging and holding trout debit a shared relative insect resource, while sunlight, air temperature, moisture and remaining resource influence visible insect activity. The insect representation is a functional guild without a claimed species identity.

Aquatic routes reference the existing named Firehole feature and a fixed range of its saved centerline points. They interpolate the authored water-surface elevation instead of sampling the river bed. A trout's activity responds to the illustrative reach temperature; **actual refuge selection is future work**. The current movement is an authored local circuit, not a population migration model, hydrodynamic swimming model, or general animal navigation system. Amphibians, reptiles, and the other catalogue species are not silently generated by this script.

The independent botanical scene adds thirty fixed clumps: six willow shrubs, twelve sedge clumps, and twelve lupine clumps. [`data/botanical_placements.json`](../data/botanical_placements.json) records each identity, metre-scale position, dimensions, habitat interpretation, and uncertainty. The original meshes emphasize narrow willow leaves on branching woody stems, arching sedge blades with small inflorescences, and palmate lupine foliage beneath flower racemes. They are genus/guild form proxies, not botanical scans or confirmation of a named plant at a particular coordinate. Their flowering state is an illustrative midsummer art choice. The 52,128-triangle detail scene should be replaced with the researched, licensed botanical assets after taxonomic and visual review.

[`tools/bake_botany.gd`](../tools/bake_botany.gd) rebuilds [`scenes/botanical_details.tscn`](../scenes/botanical_details.tscn) offline from those fixed records. It samples every individual stem base on the same terrain triangles used for walking and rejects infrastructure and thermal-water overlap. Re-run it after terrain changes. It contains no random placement or runtime habitat filling. The baked geometry can also be edited directly in Godot; retain its placement IDs and update the JSON when an edit should survive a later bake.

Verification is reproducible with `godot --headless --path . --script tools/validate_ecology.gd`. The recorded one-hour run conserves water to the reported precision, has a maximum domain heat residual of approximately 2.55 × 10⁻¹⁰ m³·°C per integration substep, and preserves a uniform 20 °C state exactly when all inputs and the atmosphere are also 20 °C. A 180-second actor check exercises the intended states, checks aquatic route confinement and bed clearance, and confirms visible feeding debits the shared patch resources. These tests establish software consistency rather than environmental accuracy. See [`tests/ecology_validation.json`](../tests/ecology_validation.json) and [`tests/botany_bake.json`](../tests/botany_bake.json); the latter includes the terrain and world-data hashes used for the bake.

## 1. The ecological identity of this segment

The strongest basis for a recognizable, convincing Midway landscape is the sharp juxtaposition of exposed silica deposits, differently colored microbial habitats, flowing water, meadow, and lodgepole forest. Grand Prismatic is a hot spring with circulating water, not a periodically erupting geyser. Its colored margins represent microbial communities; the hot water is not an aquarium. Excelsior continually supplies thermal water to the Firehole. These named features should keep their individual shapes, channels, and water levels throughout authoring. [S01][S02]

NPS gives Grand Prismatic's water-body diameter as approximately 61–101 m and depth as more than 37 m. These are reference dimensions, not permission to make a perfectly circular 101 m basin. The pool, surrounding microbial apron, and visible bare thermal ground are different extents. Aerial tracing should distinguish them. Likewise, Excelsior's approximately 61 by 91 m crater is not interchangeable with the river-facing discharge channels. [S01][S02]

**Design principle:** maximize credible differences across short distances. A sparse lodgepole stand can be more faithful than a dense mixed forest; a quiet river reach can be more faithful than continuous animal activity. Variety should emerge from soil, water, temperature, light, season, and disturbance, with a few convincing interactions connecting habitats.

### Evidence classes

Use these classes in asset placement reviews, habitat resources, and future prompts:

| Class | Meaning | Placement consequence |
|---|---|---|
| `documented_site` | A source explicitly identifies Midway or a named feature in the selected segment. | Eligible after checking the date and actual microhabitat. It still does not locate each individual. |
| `documented_corridor` | A source identifies the Firehole drainage or river; the exact patch is unspecified. | Reasonable candidate in a matching authored patch; retain uncertainty. |
| `documented_park` | A source establishes Yellowstone presence or habitat, without local confirmation. | Require local visual, herbarium, survey, or observation review before claiming an exact reconstruction. |
| `habitat_inference` | A proposed functional guild or behavior extrapolates from appropriate habitat. | Label the inference and avoid asserting a species identity that the evidence does not support. |
| `documented_elsewhere` | The evidence is from a different Yellowstone site. | Expansion reference; not sufficient to place it at Grand Prismatic. |
| `dated_nondetection` | A defined search did not find the organism at that place and time. | Record effort and date; do not convert nondetection into permanent absence. |

A public land-cover layer indicating evergreen forest is not a lodgepole census. Nor does a park checklist establish a frog breeding pond, beaver colony, raptor nest, or rare-plant patch beside the boardwalk. Retain source geometry and confidence separately from the final hand-edited geometry. An author may decide that a plausible, clearly labeled composite habitat is desirable, but that should be an explicit change in fidelity, not an accidental substitution.

## 2. Habitat layers to author before placing organisms

The catalogue uses the following stable habitat IDs. Several are potential expansion habitats rather than requirements to invent new waterbodies inside this segment.

|---|---|---|

| `forest_lodgepole` | Upland lodgepole stand | Stand-specific age, trunk spacing, deadwood, and canopy structure. |
| `forest_moist` | Independently verified moist forest patch | Different understory and possible spruce/fir candidates; do not paint this around every river segment. |
| `forest_edge` | Light and moisture transition | Irregular canopy edge, small vegetation patches, fallen wood and exposed substrate. |
| `dry_meadow` | Well-drained nonthermal herbaceous patch | Seasonal grass and forb mosaic, grazing height differences. |

| `airspace` | Connected air over appropriate feeding or resting habitat | Birds and bats use routes tied to food and perches, not generic looping swarms. |

Yellowstone's wetlands include both intermittently wet systems and permanent water. Their plant communities differ, so one generic wetland texture and one generic frog sound are insufficient. NPS specifically mentions frogs and elephant's-head flowers near the start of Firehole Lake Road, which is a valuable northern expansion reference rather than proof of an identical patch at Midway. [S06]

### Ground and root-zone constraints

Keep at least four independent physical fields: surface-water temperature, soil temperature at an explicitly named depth, soil moisture or water-table depth, and substrate class. Do not make a single distance-to-hot-spring gradient stand in for all four. A shallow warm puddle, a dry heated ridge, and a cool saturated meadow may be close together but support different organisms.

Heat can change a plant community quickly, while mature forest geometry records longer history. NPS describes thermal ground becoming lethal to plants while adjacent cooling ground becomes colonizable. This is grounds for authored dead trees and abrupt vegetation edges. It is not justification for full-grown trees disappearing and reappearing during a normal walk. [S07]

For artistic review, display each layer with a legend and unit. Draw contours for a few meaningful temperature or moisture values and show the water network over them. Inspect disconnected warm polygons, ordinary plants rooted in hot outflow, shrubs crossing a road footprint, and trees with roots visibly suspended over eroded banks before adding more vegetation.

## 3. Species choices that matter here

### Forest, meadow, and thermal plants

Lodgepole pine is the primary tree reference. Yellowstone's nutrient-poor rhyolitic settings support extensive lodgepole forest. Its appearance should include comparatively bare lower trunks within closed stands and fuller low branches on exposed individuals; those are different light histories. Spruce and subalpine fir are conditional candidates in appropriate moist, developed soils, not a universal diversity mix. [S03][S04]

Forest-floor candidates include elk sedge, grouse whortleberry, and pinegrass. Their presence in Yellowstone forest habitats supports selecting and researching these assets, but does not identify their coverage beside a particular overlook. Use current imagery and local vegetation data to resolve that next. [S03]

Fire is part of the landscape history. Distinguish surviving trees, postfire cohorts, snags, fallen logs, and later infill. Some lodgepole cones are serotinous, but not all lodgepoles carry the same cone behavior. Model the existing stand as authored geometry with a known reference date; a future fire simulation should create a separate state, not retrospectively rewrite the base scene. [S05]

Two restricted plants deserve more attention than adding many unrelated flowers:

- **Yellowstone sulphur flower**, *Eriogonum umbellatum* var. *cladophorum*, is documented in the Upper, Midway, and Lower geyser basins and toward Madison Junction. It belongs on open, relatively dry, slightly thermally influenced ground. Its yellow flowers and grayish hairy leaves offer a specific local asset target. Exact plant positions still need verification. [S08]
- **Ross's bentgrass**, *Agrostis rossiae*, is a Firehole-drainage thermal specialist with a very unusual calendar. NPS reports winter germination, its main flowering in late May and early June, and plants already dead and difficult to find by July. Keep any potential summer patch visually dormant or absent unless a dated reference supports otherwise. Hoof-mediated seed transport is a suggested mechanism, not an experimentally established universal rule. [S09]

For additional color, consider a limited set of arnica, lupine, monkeyflower, fringed gentian, elephant's head, and fireweed candidates, each tied to the correct patch and season. The catalogue avoids assuming exact species when a source gives only a genus or common-name group. NPS's wildflower dates are broad park guidance, not a promise that all these flowers overlap locally. [S10]

Do not distribute all Yellowstone megafauna across a small thermal basin. Wolves, bears, moose, pronghorn, bighorn sheep, high-elevation whitebark pine communities, and lake fish need their own geography, rarity, movement scale, and evidence. A missing species can be an accurate outcome.

## 4. Thermal ecology: represent communities, not colored paint alone

USGS identifies an approximate upper photosynthetic temperature limit of 72°C in neutral to alkaline springs; acidic systems have different constraints. Above that boundary, absence of photosynthetic mats does not mean absence of microbial life. Chemistry also matters. Therefore, do not equate “blue” with sterilized water, or “yellow” with a universal temperature class. [S33]

Separate three rendering layers:

1. **Water optics:** depth, absorption, scattering, surface reflection, and visibility of the bed.
2. **Substrate:** silica texture, deposit thickness, cracks, and submerged relief.
3. **Community appearance:** authored mat coverage, color mixture, microrelief, and slow condition-dependent changes.

A single radial color formula would erase the importance of uneven outflow. Trace the actual distribution as polygons or painted masks and retain them as authored assets. Use temperature to constrain those masks and to drive only small, slow variations until there is a calibrated succession model. Runoff direction should influence striations; stranded matte patches should differ from glossy active films. Mesh relief for mats must remain subtle at human scale.

The tiger-beetle literature shows why feature-specific evidence matters. A 2024 distribution paper lists Grand Prismatic, Excelsior, Opal Pool, and Turquoise Pool among sites without beetles during at least 15-minute inspections in late July 2016. Rabbit Creek is a documented Midway-area research site. The correct inference is a dated local distinction, not proof that beetles can never occur at those pools. [S28][S29]

A later physiological study found evidence consistent with enhanced heat reflection in Yellowstone beetles. The important implementation distinction is between a hot surface and the animal's internal body temperature: insects observed near very hot water are not equivalent to animals tolerating immersion at that temperature. Do not set insect body tolerance from a spring's vent measurement. [S34]

A striking future microscopic interpretation could show the *Dichanthelium–Curvularia–virus* association investigated experimentally for thermal tolerance. It should be a carefully sourced optional explanation attached to a verified host patch, not an invented visible organism or a claim that all thermal grasses share the association. [S35]

## 5. A small set of interacting models

The following equations are **proposed reduced models**. Their structure is physically or ecologically motivated; all example tuning coefficients are illustrative unless a measured value is explicitly identified. They are not fitted models of Grand Prismatic or the Firehole. Author-facing controls should expose units, provenance, uncertainty, and spatial scope.

### 5.1 Shared state and causality

Use persistent habitat patches and a connected river graph. A patch stores moisture, substrate, root-zone temperature, canopy openness, live forage, litter, and disturbance. A river reach stores volume, discharge, temperature, and a relative aquatic-insect index. Organisms read those fields and write a limited set of transactions such as consumption, trampling, seed removal, or predation. Rendering reads the same state, so a feeding event can influence vegetation height or insect presence.

Recommended initial update schedule, entirely a software design choice: movement and collision at the project's physics rate; near-animal decisions every 0.25–1 s; river and local weather state every 1–10 s; patch budgets hourly or daily in simulation time. Do not change thermal thresholds or grazing losses when frame rate changes. Keep an explicit simulation clock and preserve state across chunk unloads.

Use stable species, patch, reach, and individual IDs. A moving animal has one owner even while crossing a streaming boundary. Long-distance agents may remain abstract until they enter a represented area; instantiate their existing identity rather than creating a new herd each time the player turns around. The fixed world's habitat polygons and placements must never be regenerated at runtime.

### 5.2 Water, heat, and visible flow

Each reach follows a water-volume balance:

\[
\frac{dV_j}{dt}=\sum_i Q_{ij}+Q_{\mathrm{spring},j}-Q_{\mathrm{out},j}-E_j-I_j.
\]

Here \(V\) is m³; all \(Q\), evaporation \(E\), and infiltration \(I\) terms are m³/s. Use explicit boundary inflow where the Firehole enters the scene. A contained map is not the complete catchment. Ordinary rainfall on a few square kilometers must not be expected to supply all observed river flow.

For a well-mixed reach, a simplified heat budget is:

\[
\frac{dT_j}{dt}=\frac{\sum_i Q_{ij}(T_i-T_j)+Q_{\mathrm{spring},j}(T_{\mathrm{spring},j}-T_j)}{V_j}
+\frac{A_j H_{\mathrm{net},j}}{\rho c_p V_j}.
\]

\(H_{\rm net}\) is net heat flux in W/m², \(A\) surface area in m², \(\rho\) water density, and \(c_p\) specific heat. Assumptions include mixed compartments and approximately constant material properties. Strong near-outfall gradients violate the well-mixed assumption, so represent the discharge and plume as smaller connected cells before merging with the wider river.

NPS's Excelsior discharge description, more than 4,000 US gallons per minute, corresponds to more than approximately **0.252 m³/s**. That conversion is a useful order-of-magnitude constraint, not a current flow measurement for every outlet. Divide the total among observed outlets, cool it along the actual channels, and preserve its connection to the Firehole. [S02]

As an inexpensive steady outflow approximation:

\[
T(x)=T_a+(T_0-T_a)\exp\left(-\frac{h_e b x}{\rho c_p Q}\right).
\]

Here \(h_e\) is an effective heat-transfer coefficient in W/(m² K), \(b\) exposed channel width, and \(x\) downstream distance. This ignores several important processes, including latent heat, lateral infiltration, and changing width. It is suitable as a clearly labeled authoring approximation, not a substitute for measured channel temperatures. Do not invent a universal value for \(h_e\).

Use the same flow direction in water normals, foam transport, floating detritus, aquatic-insect drift, and fish orientation. Keep bed elevation below the water surface, connect tributary and outfall endpoints, and avoid gaps between water meshes. River temperature, surface color, and fish suitability should be linked but not identical: clear water can be dangerously hot, and a cool-looking material is not a thermometer.

USGS maintains Firehole monitoring locations at Old Faithful and near West Yellowstone. Their location and upstream catchment differ from Midway. Future calibration should use their time series as boundary and comparison data, with spatial offsets explicitly documented. Never label a downstream gauge reading as the current temperature beside the Midway footbridge. [S36][S37]

### 5.3 Vegetation, moisture, and herbivory

For an authored patch, a compact water store in millimeters can use:

\[
\frac{dW}{dt}=P+R+G-ET-D.
\]

Rain/snowmelt \(P\), run-on \(R\), groundwater exchange \(G\), evapotranspiration \(ET\), and drainage \(D\) must all use compatible units. Clamp only after recording any overflow or unmet loss. Keeping a hidden residual or simply clamping away excess water makes cross-patch comparisons difficult to audit.

A nonnegative forage model is:

\[
\frac{dB}{dt}=r\,f_T\,f_W\,f_L\,B\left(1-\frac{B}{K}\right)-C-H,
\]

where \(B\) is live biomass, \(K\) patch carrying biomass, \(C\) consumption, and \(H\) senescence. Temperature, moisture, and light multipliers are dimensionless. All are illustrative until calibrated. For now, consider \(B\) and \(K\) relative indices rather than pretending to know local kg/m². Vegetation cannot be consumed below zero; a successful grazing transaction removes the same quantity that the herbivore receives.

Keep forage quality separate from quantity. Recently grazed vegetation can be short and attractive without being abundant. Bison's repeated use of grazing areas and their seasonal movements provide a research basis for exploring that distinction, while local growth and stocking rates still require calibration. [S11]

Trampling can reduce standing height and expose small patches independently of consumption. Place persistent wallows or strongly worn crossings only from imagery or approved authored interpretation. Avoid making every hoofprint erase a perfectly circular patch of grass.

### 5.4 Habitat suitability without procedural world generation

For species \(s\) in an already authored patch at position \(x\), define an eligibility gate \(G_s(x)\) and a suitability score:

\[
S_s(x)=G_s(x)\prod_k f_{s,k}(x),\qquad 0\leq S_s\leq1.
\]

A hard gate excludes road footprints, hot cores, incompatible water, or absent required substrate. Softer factors rank moisture, cover, sunlight, and food. The product is deliberately conservative: excellent food does not compensate for lethal temperature. A weighted-sum alternative is useful for preference but should be applied only after hard exclusions.

If the editor includes mathematical placement assistance, use it to preview **candidates**. A spatially varying hard-core point process can suggest tree spacing; clustered patch proposals can suggest clonal groundcover. An author must approve, move, delete, and serialize the resulting transforms. Record proposal seed only for reproducibility of the editing session. The shipped scene loads accepted transforms and never samples a new forest.

Review spacing at stand scale. A universal minimum distance produces a tidy plantation effect. Let actual crown architecture, stem cohorts, deadwood, and mapped clearings set separate local constraints. Do not force every asset type to occur in every chunk, and do not use streaming tile boundaries as ecological boundaries.

### 5.5 Insects, fish, and bird behavior

A useful small food web consists of an aquatic-insect resource, introduced trout, and dippers. Use an emergence index influenced by date, water temperature, and source reach. An optional accumulated-temperature clock is:

\[
DD_{d+1}=DD_d+\max(0,\overline{T}_d-T_b).
\]

\(DD\) is degree-days and \(T_b\) a taxon-specific developmental base. Neither a universal base nor a universal emergence threshold is supplied here; both require an identified insect taxon and literature or local records. A calendar-authored emergence curve is more honest than an unvalidated degree-day model disguised as measurement.

For a fish choosing among connected cells, use:

\[
U_i=w_fF_i+w_cC_i-w_t\phi(T_i)-w_vV_i-w_dD_i,
\]

where food \(F\), cover \(C\), thermal stress \(\phi\), swimming cost \(V\), and disturbance \(D\) are normalized inputs. Require a traversable path to the chosen cell and a maximum travel budget. Thermal stress should accumulate with exposure and recovery; avoid a binary alive/dead switch at 20°C.

A Firehole study documented summer use of Sentinel Creek as a cold-water refuge. That is evidence for behavior and the importance of tributary connectivity, not evidence of a cold refuge inside the selected Midway reach. In hot scenarios, fewer fish may remain visible in the map. NPS reported thermally stressful conditions and fish deaths in the Firehole during 2026; the historical trout literature must not be interpreted as immunity to heat. [S23][S38]

Dippers should alternate perch, look, dive, emerge, preen, and rest states. The perch and dive point belong to a reach and must track its water level. A dive can reduce the same local insect resource used by fish, scaled appropriately for the abstraction. Avoid perpetual successful feeding or identical dive intervals. The ecological relationship is observable through repeated use of plausible foraging locations, not through floating status labels. [S15]

### 5.6 Terrestrial foraging and pollination

Voles provide a useful mostly hidden prey state. A coyote may listen, reorient, make a short pounce, then search elsewhere. A failed pounce should be possible; success consumes a tracked prey unit and should not happen every few seconds. The relationship is supported by Yellowstone species accounts, but encounter rates and trajectories are authoring parameters. [S20][S21]

A simple nectar budget can connect flowering patches and pollinator visits:

\[
\frac{dN}{dt}=R_N(T,L,B_f)-\sum_a c_a,
\]

where \(B_f\) is flowering biomass and \(N\) is a relative nectar resource. Plant-specific compatibility matters: visiting any flower is not automatically successful pollination. Until local pollinator identities are resolved, label the actors as a guild and treat pollination success as an experimental extension. NPS's own Yellowstone pollinator work records weather, vegetation, and bloom timing together, providing a useful observation schema. [S39]

For nighttime expansion, bat activity should depend on insect availability and weather, with roosts offscreen where appropriate. Do not fill the daytime steam with bat silhouettes or use the same sound loop for every species. Ultrasound is not normally an audible atmospheric sound; an optional detector mode would be a separate future interaction. [S30]

## 6. What must remain separate from the first build

### Long-term geomorphology and trophic cascades

Willow recovery cannot be implemented as `wolf_count * tree_growth`. A decade-long Yellowstone experiment found that reducing browsing alone was insufficient to restore studied small-stream willow communities; hydrological conditions also mattered. That experiment concerns northern-range riparian systems, not Grand Prismatic. It supports a multi-factor design and cautions against transferring a famous simplified narrative to this site. [S40]

A future beaver system needs connected channels, low-gradient suitable reaches, food, and altered stage/storage relationships. It should affect pond geometry, groundwater, sediment, and movement paths together. Do not add a beaver dam to the Firehole simply to demonstrate the code. Without local evidence, develop it in a separately identified expansion habitat. NPS describes both dam-building and river-bank dens, so “beaver present” does not imply “dam spanning river.” [S31]

### Introduced species and chemical exposure

The Firehole's contemporary fish community has a stocking history. Keep origin separate from aesthetic appeal; a nonnative fish can be part of a faithful contemporary scene. New Zealand mudsnails are also documented in Firehole studies, where researchers examined their large contribution to invertebrate production. This supports a future competing benthic-resource pool, but measured rates from one study must not be copied onto every present-day reach. [S22][S41]

Geothermal environments also involve chemical exposure. Work on Rabbit Creek and Norris thermal food chains makes metal transfer a possible research extension. Represent it initially as metadata or a controlled educational scenario. Do not invent acute visible poisoning, classify all thermal drainage as equivalent, or imply that a bright mat color measures toxicity. [S29]

## 7. Authoring, calibration, and review workflow

**First select a reference season and year.** A representative summer snapshot is a valid artistic choice, but label it as such. A date-specific reconstruction requires matching photos, vegetation condition, hydrology, infrastructure, and wildlife evidence. For the first summer scene, use the date as a phenology input while keeping day/night progression independent of seasonal progression. A 20-minute walk should not grow a new tree or transform spring into fall.

Author the river and named pool boundaries before species placement. Check DEM-derived banks against imagery; shallow thermal channels often need deliberately sculpted detail beyond coarse elevation data. Store root and water-surface heights explicitly. Surface textures must not create apparent waterfalls where the collision and bed model have no drop. Where two chunks meet, share boundary samples and continuous habitat coordinates so both sides read the same water and moisture state.

Next, divide the forest into recognizable stands rather than scattering a single pine across the entire tile. Use a few strong reference trees, representative smaller cohorts, and deadwood groups. Inspect the silhouettes from the road, boardwalk, bridge, and overlook. Only after these forms read correctly should more understory, insects, and fine surface detail be added.

Create each wildlife route as an authored graph of eligible feeding, resting, and transit locations. Specify where it may leave the map. An animal route must not cross the road centerline unless a crossing is deliberate, and it must not pass through thermal cores because a navigation bake accidentally joined their banks. Use species-specific exclusions in addition to physical collision; what a player can walk across is not necessarily habitat a frog or fish can occupy.

Keep **observed data**, **derived values**, and **design parameters** in separate fields. Examples: a source discharge range is observed-reference data; converting gallons/minute to m³/s is a derivation; choosing ten visual insect instances is a design parameter. Include the original source unit. Store a confidence label and reference date for every patch where a rare species is claimed.

A compact calibration sheet should include:

- Reference image or survey, location precision, date, season, viewpoint, and reuse permission.
- Habitat polygon, substrate interpretation, temperature measurement depth, and whether moisture is observed or inferred.
- Species or guild, evidence class, life stage, phenology, and local abundance if measured.
- Water reach, upstream/downstream connection, flow/temperature observations, uncertainty, and data gaps.
- Asset identity, approximate dimensions, approved local appearance, seasonal variants, and animation limitations.
- Each model parameter's unit, source or rationale, calibrated range, and behavior outside that range.

Meaningful validation follows the causal chains. In a hotter river scenario, thermal suitability changes before fish relocate, and fish cannot select a disconnected refuge. Removing wetland water silences a breeding chorus according to a defined rule rather than instantly spawning frogs elsewhere. A grazed patch loses biomass and changes appearance consistently. Reloading a scene preserves those states. These tests target the behavior that the world is meant to communicate.

For visual review, use matched cameras at several named viewpoints and a few ground-level inspection points. Inspect shadows and root contact at midday, waterline continuity at low viewing angles, steam against dark trees, and vegetation edges from above. Ecological accuracy and graphical beauty should reinforce one another: a wet outfall needs convincing moisture, and a pine rooted in a barren pool remains wrong even with excellent bark textures.

## 8. Expansion sequence and implementation prompts

### A. Finish Midway's local evidence and seasonality

Highest value next work: obtain dated close-range reference for plant communities, map actual mat/outflow boundaries, validate tree cohorts, identify river reaches with plausible fish observation points, and collect or license spatially appropriate audio. Do not claim a sound recording is unrestricted merely because NPS hosts it; partner credits and individual media terms still need checking. The Firehole dipper recording is an especially relevant reference. [S16]

**Prompt:** “Preserve the existing meter-scale Midway geometry and authored IDs. Audit every plant and animal patch against `species_catalogue.json`. Produce a confidence map and a change list; do not convert corridor-level evidence into point observations. Add season variants for approved patches, especially winter/early-spring Ross's bentgrass and summer sulphur flower. Keep simulation coefficients labeled as illustrative until fitted.”

### B. Extend toward Rabbit Creek and the Lower Geyser Basin

This extension adds a documented insect study context and different thermal microhabitats. Keep specific research sites and public visitor routes distinct; do not treat a research location as an invented public footpath. Thermal maps, current imagery, and local channel connections should lead the geometry. Firehole Lake Road offers an independently documented wetland reference, allowing a richer amphibian/flower soundscape without moving that habitat to Grand Prismatic. [S06][S29]

**Prompt:** “Extend the authored scene northward using actual road, water, and elevation data. Preserve scale and seamless boundaries. Add only source-supported Rabbit Creek insect habitats, retain the 2016 Grand Prismatic nondetection record, and implement separate surface, water, and insect-body temperatures. Show food-web effects through local fly and beetle activity. Include a source-to-patch audit.”

### C. Follow the Firehole toward Upper Geyser Basin

A longer corridor provides meaningful inflow/outflow continuity and better scope for thermal refuges, multiple forest ages, and more complex bird routes. Infrastructure and thermal features can change; select a reference date before adding Biscuit Basin or other affected sites. The existing scene must remain one authored geographic world, with new chunks aligned to the same origin and units. [S42]

**Prompt:** “Add the next contiguous Firehole corridor segment without moving existing landmarks. Distinguish ordinary tributary water, hydrothermal discharge, and river mixing. Ingest monitoring records with gauge location and time metadata. Fit only parameters supported by those data, retain measured and simulated series separately, and verify that temperature-driven fish movement follows connected water.”

### D. Add geographically distinct park regions

A northern-range extension can support sagebrush, larger ungulate landscapes, willows, and carefully modeled predator–prey questions. Yellowstone Lake is the appropriate setting for a separate lake-fish and waterbird system. Higher elevations are the context for whitebark-pine interactions. These should be new contiguous regions or explicit travel destinations; shrinking the intervening geography would break the project's scale promise. [S04][S22][S43]

**Prompt:** “Choose one new Yellowstone region and state its ecological differences from Midway. Use primary sources to build a new habitat catalogue and dependency graph before adding species. Preserve the fixed-world authoring method. Explain the travel and streaming boundary, data resolution, and the role of each new organism. Do not add iconic species solely to increase species count.”

### E. Optional player observation tools

Useful later interactions include binocular inspection, a field notebook with confidence labels, matched historical photographs, an optional river-temperature overlay, a directional microphone, and a time-lapse camera. None requires combat, collection, or progression. A thermal interpretation mode could expose the hidden cause of mat boundaries; a bird notebook could record behavior rather than rewarding proximity.

**Prompt:** “Add observation tools that read existing ecosystem state. A notebook observation must record time, location, species confidence, and behavior; do not claim that a player action discovers a species if the model only represents a guild. Keep temperature values marked simulated. Use the same fish, water, forage, and insect state as the visible world, and keep the ordinary exploration view free of debug labels.”
