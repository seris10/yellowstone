## Abstract

Ten studies spanning grizzly bear demography, wolf–elk predator–prey dynamics, trophic cascades, coyote territorial behavior, bison disease ecology, and aquatic invasive species collectively provide a quantitatively rich foundation for a multi-species Yellowstone ecological simulation in Python. Wolf density on the Northern Range scales predictably with elk prey biomass (BMI = elk/km² × 3; observed 54 vs. predicted 55 wolves/1,000 km²; r² = 0.81, P \< 0.0001)[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015], and data-driven sparse regression applied to 1995–2022 population time series reveals that the wolf–elk system exhibits saddle-node and Hopf bifurcations separating zones of stable coexistence, limit cycles, and extinction[N. Coyote territories are mechanistically explainable by prey-resource distributions and conspecific pack avoidance rather than terrain, with a fitted PDE-derived home-range model predicting territorial shifts after pack dissolution (ΔAIC = 39.6)[P. Bison brucellosis follows a density-dependent SI dynamic with an establishment threshold of approximately 200 bison, above which eradication by culling is practically infeasible[A. Dobson & M. # Thematic Analysis

## Theme 1: Wolf–Elk Predator–Prey Population Dynamics and Mathematical Structure

The wolf–elk interaction is the most formally modeled relationship in the reviewed literature. Using yearly Northern Yellowstone Park population data from 1995 to 2022, Kumari et al. (2025) constructed a data-driven model via Gaussian process regression for smoothing, sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (E-SINDy), and model selection[N. Kumari et al., 2025]. The resulting system was analyzed for nonlinear dynamics, and stability and bifurcation analyses were performed to describe qualitative behavior[N. Kumari et al., 2025]. The model identified three qualitatively distinct dynamical zones: stable coexistence, periodic oscillations (limit cycles), and extinction scenarios[N. Kumari et al., 2025]. A saddle-node bifurcation identifies parameter ranges permitting elk–wolf coexistence; outside these ranges, one or both populations may go extinct[N. Kumari et al., 2025]. Hopf bifurcations delineate the boundary between stable equilibria and oscillatory behavior, while codimension-two bifurcations (Bogdanov-Takens and cusp) emerge when two parameters vary simultaneously[N. Kumari et al., 2025]. Ecologically, these bifurcations reflect the interplay between wolf predation pressure and elk defense mechanisms such as grouping or herd behavior, and small parameter changes can shift the system between stable coexistence and oscillatory or extinction outcomes[N. Kumari et al., 2025].

Independently, Mech & Barber-Meyer (2015) validated a prey-biomass–wolf-density model for the Northern Range using 2005–2012 data, after elk population fluctuations dampened approximately 10 years post-reintroduction[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015]. They found that Northern Range wolf density conformed tightly to the established prey-biomass model: observed mean wolf density was 54 wolves/1,000 km² at a prey biomass index (BMI) of 14.98 kg/km², compared with the model's prediction of 55 wolves/1,000 km²[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015]. The model fit improved from r² = 0.79 to r² = 0.81 (P \< 0.0001) and was robust to BMI perturbations of ±20% and to using full-range wolf density estimates[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015]. The prey biomass index itself was computed as elk density (elk/km²) multiplied by a relative biomass equivalent factor of 3, with other prey species incorporated at their respective biomass equivalents[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015]. Taken together, the Mech & Barber-Meyer model provides a parametric steady-state scaling relationship between predator density and prey biomass, while the Kumari et al. framework provides the dynamical structure governing oscillations, coexistence, and collapse—complementary tools for a simulation.

Creel et al. (2007) provide a mechanistic behavioral link between wolf presence and elk demography, distinct from direct predation mortality. Across five elk populations and 16 site years, fecal progesterone concentrations declined with decreasing elk-to-wolf ratio, and progesterone concentrations were a significant predictor of calf recruitment in the subsequent year[S. Creel et al., 2007]. The authors interpret this as wolves indirectly reducing elk reproductive output through the physiological costs of sustained antipredator behavior[S. Creel et al., 2007]. This nontrophic, stress-mediated pathway means that a simulation using only per-capita predation rates will underestimate total wolf effects on elk population growth; an additional term representing reproductive suppression as a function of predation risk (operationalized as wolf-to-elk ratio) is warranted.

## Theme 2: Trophic Cascades — Wolves, Elk, Plants, and Bears

Multiple studies document cascading effects of wolf predation on lower trophic levels, providing both qualitative evidence and quantitative before–after comparisons usable in a multi-trophic simulation module.

Beyer et al. (2007) quantified willow (*Salix boothii* and *S. geyeriana*) stem growth on Yellowstone's northern winter range, comparing pre- and post-wolf-reintroduction periods[H. Beyer et al., 2007]. Annual growth-ring area approximately doubled after wolf reintroduction for both species[H. Beyer et al., 2007]. Wolf presence was a significant predictor of stem growth beyond climate and hydrological variables, and stem growth was inversely related to the preceding winter's percentage of twigs browsed[H. Beyer et al., 2007]. Annual model selection favored wolf-presence models over models using only elk population size or excluding wolves entirely[H. Beyer et al., 2007]. This implies that the pathway operates at least partly through behavioral modification of elk space use—a "landscape of fear" effect—rather than solely through elk abundance reduction.

Ripple et al. (2014) extended the cascade to the grizzly bear trophic level, reporting that fruit frequency in grizzly bear scats was inversely correlated (P \< 0.001) with elk population size over 19 years and 6,231 scats[W. Ripple et al., 2014]. After wolf reintroduction, average fruit percentage in July increased from 0.3% to 5.9% and in August from 7.8% to 14.6%[W. Ripple et al., 2014]. Serviceberry (*Amelanchier alnifolia*) stems accessible to ungulates outside an exclosure all originated post-wolf-reintroduction, while protected stems in the exclosure originated both before and after, indicating that ungulate browsing suppressed serviceberry establishment prior to wolf reintroduction[W. Ripple et al., 2014]. In recent years, browsing outside the exclosure decreased and plant heights increased[W. Ripple et al., 2014].

Together these studies indicate that a simulation attempting ecological realism for the northern Yellowstone range should model at least four trophic levels: wolves → elk → shrub/berry biomass → grizzly bear foraging success. The intermediate plant trophic level is governed by the inverse relationship between elk browse pressure and shrub growth rate[H. Beyer et al., 2007], with wolf presence modifying the magnitude of that browse pressure beyond what elk abundance alone predicts.

## Theme 4: Climate–Predation Interactions and Scavenger Carrion Dynamics

Wilmers & Getz (2005) quantify the interactive effects of wolf predation and climate change on carrion availability for the broader scavenger guild[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005]. Analysis of 55 years of Yellowstone weather data showed that winters are becoming shorter, with more January–March days above freezing, declining midwinter snowfall, and a later snow-cover season[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005]. These trends reduce late-winter elk mortality from starvation and exposure, which traditionally subsidized scavengers such as ravens, eagles, and coyotes.

Using an empirically derived scenario model comparing trajectories with and without wolves, the authors found that modeled reductions in March carrion were 4% with wolves vs. 27% without wolves, and April reductions were 11% with wolves vs. 66% without wolves[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005]. Wolves thus effectively buffer approximately 55–60 percentage points of the late-winter carrion deficit caused by shorter winters[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005]. Without wolves, earlier snow thaw causes a substantial reduction in late-winter carrion and favors scavengers capable of tracking widely dispersed food sources over large distances rather than relying on localized winter kill[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005].

## Theme 5: Coyote Territorial Dynamics and Mechanistic Home Range Modeling

Moorcroft et al. (2006) analyzed coyote space-use in Yellowstone's Lamar Valley using mechanistic home-range models derived from correlated random walk movement models[P. Moorcroft et al., 2006]. Rather than resource selection analysis—a spatially implicit approach—this framework yields spatially explicit predictions of utilization distributions (modeled as two-dimensional probability density functions) fitted to 1,955 coyote relocations[P. Moorcroft et al., 2006]. Model comparison (by AIC) showed that responses to the spatial distribution of prey resources, combined with avoidance of neighboring pack territories, better explained observed space-use than did avoidance of steep terrain[P. Moorcroft et al., 2006]. The model fit improved by ΔAIC = 39.6 after accounting for the dissolution of the Norris pack, and the fitted model correctly predicted subsequent shifts in neighboring packs' territorial expansions into the vacated Norris territory[P. Moorcroft et al., 2006].

For simulation purposes, this mechanistic framework is directly implementable in Python as a partial differential equation (PDE) or agent-based movement model where individual movement is governed by a biased correlated random walk, with attraction toward high-prey-density cells and repulsion from cells with high conspecific pack intensity[P. Moorcroft et al., 2006; P. Moorcroft et al., 2006]. The model predicts emergent territorial boundaries and steady-state utilization distributions from these local movement rules, without requiring territories to be defined a priori. The prey-distribution and conspecific-avoidance parameters can be estimated from the Yellowstone data, and wolf–coyote competitive suppression (a well-established interaction in the literature, though addressed in the Wilmers & Getz carrion context[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005]) can be incorporated as a mortality or displacement term in the coyote sub-model.

## Theme 6: Brucellosis in Bison — Host–Pathogen Dynamics

Dobson & Meagher (1996) apply a mathematical model to brucellosis (*Brucella abortus*) dynamics in the Yellowstone bison herd[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996]. The model identifies a relatively sharp threshold for pathogen establishment and shows that the proportion of the host population infected increases as a function of population density above that threshold[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996]. The threshold herd size for *Brucella* establishment is approximately 200 bison—a level the Yellowstone population has long exceeded—making eradication from a wild population practically infeasible[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996]. Calculations using observed prevalence and recruitment data indicate that culling sufficient to eradicate brucellosis would require reductions to unacceptably low population levels[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996].

This framework maps directly onto a standard susceptible–infected (SI) or susceptible–infected–recovered (SIR) compartmental model with a density-dependent force of infection. The relevant terms for a Python implementation are:

- A threshold host density *N*\* ≈ 200 bison for pathogen invasion[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996]
- A density-dependent transmission term β·S·I/N or β·S·I (frequency- vs. density-dependent), parameterized such that the basic reproduction number R₀ \> 1 above the threshold[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996]
- Prevalence increasing as a monotonic function of density above *N*\*[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996]

## Theme 7: Aquatic Invasive Species — Lake Trout and Cutthroat Trout

Ruzycki et al. (2003) address the introduction of lake trout (*Salvelinus namaycush*) into Yellowstone Lake and its impact on the native Yellowstone cutthroat trout (*Oncorhynchus clarki bouvieri*)[J. The study applied a bioenergetics model incorporating lake trout diets, thermal history, growth, and size structure to estimate predatory impact, and it evaluated the effectiveness of the NPS lake trout control program[J. Ruzycki et al., 2003]. Lake trout abundance was estimated using virtual population (cohort) analysis, and cutthroat trout abundance was estimated using hydroacoustics[J. Ruzycki et al., 2003].

# Synthesis

The ten studies collectively reveal a Yellowstone ecosystem in which wolf predation functions simultaneously as a direct population-regulatory force on elk, an indirect regulator of riparian vegetation and berry-producing shrubs, a behavioral suppressor of elk reproductive physiology, a carrion subsidy for scavengers, and a competitive suppressor of coyote space use. These cascading effects operate across at least four trophic levels[H. Beyer et al., 2007; W. Ripple et al., 2014; C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005; P. Moorcroft et al., 2006], and their quantitative magnitudes are substantial enough to parameterize in a simulation without implausible extrapolation.

Two apparent tensions in the evidence warrant explicit reconciliation for simulation design. First, Mech & Barber-Meyer (2015) argue that prey biomass ultimately governs wolf density[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015], implying a top-down cascade driven by a bottom-up regulated predator population. Kumari et al. (2025) identify saddle-node and Hopf bifurcations that permit extinction or limit cycles at parameter values outside the coexistence zone[N. Kumari et al., 2025], and Creel et al. (2007) show that wolf presence reduces elk fecundity independently of abundance[S. Creel et al., 2007]. These findings are not contradictory: the prey-biomass model describes the long-run equilibrium wolf density, while bifurcation analysis and behavioral suppression describe transient dynamics and mechanisms by which the system can be displaced from or attracted to that equilibrium. A simulation should implement both a carrying-capacity relationship linking prey biomass to wolf density at equilibrium[L. D. Mech & S. Barber-Meyer, 2015] and a dynamic equation structure that permits limit cycles and extinction when parameters (e.g., prey defense, wolf offtake) shift outside the coexistence range[N. Kumari et al., 2025].

Second, grizzly bear demographic sensitivity is strongly concentrated in independent female survival rather than cub or yearling survival[C. C. Schwartz et al., 2006], but the trophic cascade studies show that wolf-mediated changes in berry availability may improve grizzly condition and ultimately affect female survival and reproduction indirectly[W. Ripple et al., 2014]. A simulation that treats these as independent modules will underestimate the systemic effect of wolves on the grizzly population; linking the plant module's berry output to a grizzly-condition state variable, and that condition variable to survival probability, would better capture the documented relationship[W. Ripple et al., 2014; C. C. Schwartz et al., 2006].

Third, both bison brucellosis[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996] and lake trout invasion[J. Ruzycki et al., 2003] represent density-dependent processes that are largely decoupled from the wolf–elk–plant cascade but interact with it through shared host populations (bison and cutthroat trout feed grizzly bears, eagles, and other predators). A complete simulation should implement these as semi-independent modules with outputs (bison abundance, cutthroat trout abundance) feeding into shared consumer state variables, but with disease and invasion dynamics governed by their own threshold structures[A. Dobson & M. Meagher, 1996; J. Ruzycki et al., 2003].

Finally, the shortening-winter trend documented by Wilmers & Getz (2005)[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005] constitutes a time-varying forcing term that affects elk winter mortality, carrion availability, and grizzly bear foraging success. A Python simulation intended for ecological robustness should implement winter severity as a stochastic or trend-driven environmental variable rather than a fixed parameter, with downstream effects on carrion biomass[C. Wilmers & W. Getz, 2005], grizzly bear survival[C. C. Schwartz et al., 2006], and willow growth[H. Beyer et al., 2007] all parameterized as functions of that shared index.
