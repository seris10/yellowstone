# Yellowstone **Insect Simulation** Framework

The attached Yellowstone audit identifies **Priority 1 simulation taxa** as Ephemerella subvaria, Pteronarcys californica, Aeshna spp., Baetidae/Baetis spp., Formica spp., Polistes spp., Libellula spp., and Colias philodice, and it specifies the needed parameter classes: habitat, phenology, thermal limits, interaction strengths, and recovery-style resource dynamics for `insects_birds.py` integration . The same attached document also notes Yellowstone-scale richness targets of **400+ mayflies, 120+ stoneflies, 300+ caddisflies, 150+ odonates, and 500+ butterflies**, which argues for a guild-plus-species architecture rather than full individual species parameterization at first pass .

## Core State Variables

Use a **species or guild state vector** with abundance \(N_i\), activity \(A_i\), stage \(L_i\), and habitat suitability \(H_i\) for each taxon, because the attached plan explicitly requires activity functions, phenology-stage mappings, thermal tolerance, and recovery-style abundance dynamics . For Yellowstone implementation, separate at minimum into **aquatic EPT**, **aquatic predators**, **odonates**, **bumble bees**, and **terrestrial social insects**, because the attached species list and prioritization divide taxa along those ecological functions .

A practical abundance equation is:
\[
\frac{dN_i}{dt}=r_i N_i\left(1-\frac{N_i}{K_i}\right)-\sum_j a_{ji}N_jN_i-m_iN_i
\]
because stream insect community models with **logistic prey and Lotka–Volterra-type predation** produced feasible carrying capacities and stable equilibria in a four-species system (Speirs et al., 2000). If spatial patches are used, add diffusion or movement terms only where justified, since reaction-diffusion predator–prey models can stabilize coexistence but also add assumptions not yet Yellowstone-calibrated (Wang et al., 2026).

## Thermal And Phenology Equations

For **activity**, use a bounded multiplicative form already requested by the attached audit:
\[
A_i=\sigma(\beta_T f_T(T)+\beta_W f_W(W)+\beta_M f_M(M)+\beta_S f_S(S)+\beta_H f_H(h))
\]
where \(T\)=air or water temperature, \(W\)=wind or flow stress, \(M\)=moisture, \(S\)=sun/cloud, and \(h\)=hour/circadian state . This structure is biologically defensible because insect activity, metabolism, growth, and development are temperature-dependent across ectotherms, and bumble bee phenology is also shaped by snowmelt and seasonal temperature windows (Kingsolver & Woods, 2016; González et al., 2022; Miller-Struttmann et al., 2022).

For **thermal performance**, use unimodal thermal performance curves with \(T_{\min}\), \(T_{\opt}\), and \(T_{\max}\), because these are standard metrics for ectotherm rates and are explicitly described for insect performance modeling (Abarca et al., 2024). A simulation-safe generic form is:
\[
P_i(T)=\max\left(0,\frac{(T-T_{\min,i}}{T_{\opt,i}-T_{\min,i}} \exp\left[1-\frac{T-T_{\min,i}}{T_{\opt,i}-T_{\min,i}}\right]\right)
\]
with an enforced cutoff above \(T_{\max,i}\), because the literature supports unimodal, left-skewed performance rather than linear temperature responses (Abarca et al., 2024; Stabentheiner, 2026). Constant-temperature TPCs should not be used naively under field fluctuations, because hourly summing often underestimated real development under cold excursions and time-dependent heat stress shifts optimum and mean performance downward with longer exposure (Khelifa et al., 2019; Kingsolver & Woods, 2016).

For **phenology**, growing-degree structure is appropriate:
\[
GDD_i(t)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\max(0,T_\tau-T_{0,i})
\]
because degree-day style thresholds derive from the rising limb of development curves and are widely used for phenological prediction (Abarca et al., 2024). But Yellowstone alpine insects also require a **snowmelt gate** or floral-resource gate, because earlier snowmelt advanced flowering and increased flower–bee mismatch, while worker peaks could remain comparatively fixed among years (Kudo et al., 2025). ## Yellowstone-Relevant Taxa

| Taxon/guild | Model-ready findings | Simulation implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Pteronarcys californica** | Flowing water increased heat tolerance by **~4°C** and lowered oxygen tolerance threshold by **~15% O2** relative to standing water (Frakes et al., 2021) | Couple CTmax to velocity and DO |
| **Pteronarcys californica** | High temperature under low flow cut survival **6.5×** in a 45-day drought mesocosm (Albertson et al., 2026) | Add temperature × low-flow mortality term |
| **Pteronarcys californica** | Copper exposure reduced CTmax by **~10°C** in some cases and worsened hypoxia tolerance by **~0.5 mg/L DO** (Frakes et al., 2022) | Add pollutant stress modifier |
| **Pteronarcys californica** | Warm acclimation did not raise CTmax, but hypoxia acclimation raised CTmax by **~1°C** (Malison et al., 2022) | Allow limited acclimation plasticity |
| **Bombus spp.** | High-elevation bees tolerated colder minima, while low-altitude bees tolerated **~5°C hotter** air maxima (Giri & Dillon, 2016) | Parameterize CTmin/CTmax by elevation |
| **Bombus spp.** | Earlier flowering under warming and snowmelt shifts increased mismatch; **1°C warming + 10 earlier snowmelt days** shortened flowering by **9.2 days** (Kudo et al., 2025) | Couple bee foraging success to floral window |

## Key Modeling Rules

- **Aquatic insect abundance** should respond to hydrology and predator structure, because long-term monitoring linked densities to hydroperiod and fish assemblages, and other stream experiments linked prey density to predator pressure (Pintar & Dorn, 2026; Alexander et al., 2016).
- **Predation terms** should be taxon-specific, because predator impacts were uneven across prey species in stream models and Baetis density was strongly reduced by stonefly predation (Speirs et al., 2000; Alexander et al., 2016).
- **Parasitism and delayed density dependence** deserve optional hooks, because aquatic insects can show multi-generation cycles driven by host-killing parasites with one-generation lags (Lancaster & Downes, 2018).
- **Bumble bee abundance** should depend on both direct climate and host-plant timing, because flowering advances with warmer springs while alpine bee community shifts appear partly mediated by floral turnover and partly by warming-tolerant colonizers (Miller-Struttmann et al., 2022; Sponsler et al., 2022).

For a Yellowstone Python sim, the strongest evidence supports starting with **guild-structured differential equations plus species-specific thermal and phenology modifiers**, especially for salmonflies, Baetis/Baetidae, and bumble bees. The attached audit’s own requirements fit that architecture closely, and the broader literature shows that **flow, oxygen, snowmelt, thermal variability, predation, and floral timing** are the highest-value drivers to encode first (Verhille et al., 2024; Kingsolver & Woods, 2016; Pintar & Dorn, 2026).
